\begin{frame}{Question and Answer}

\begin{block}{Question}
Which dynamic specification best explains and forecasts Spain's year-on-year real GDP growth?
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Answer in one line}
The selected BVAR(2) is the best individual econometric model; an equal-weight forecast combination provides competitive accuracy and dampens model-specific overreactions.
\end{block}

\vspace{0.5cm}
\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Models compared:} BVAR(2) (preferred), AR(2), VAR(2), ARIMA (BIC-selected), and an equal-weight forecast combination.
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{(Preliminary) Diagnostic Checks: Stationarity}
\label{slide:stationarity}
\footnotesize
Table~\ref{tab:stationarity} reports ADF (null: unit root) and KPSS (null: level stationarity) tests. We combine the two so that conclusions agree only when both procedures reject in the appropriate direction.

{\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{5pt}
\input{../output/tables_q/unit_roots_q.tex}
}

\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GDP growth shows mixed formal unit-root evidence.} The ADF test does not reject a unit root at 5\%, while KPSS rejects level stationarity. However, visually and economically, year-on-year growth is mean-reverting. We proceed treating $\Delta y_t$ as $I(0)$, a standard macroeconometric choice.

  \item \textbf{Inflation and the long-term rate} display high persistence, with KPSS strongly rejecting stationarity for both. Following the macroeconomic literature, we keep the variables in \textit{levels} rather than differencing them, since (a) the Bayesian VAR's Minnesota prior shrinks coefficients toward a random walk and accommodates persistent regressors, and (b) cointegration with growth is plausible and economically meaningful.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

% SLIDE 9: Optimal Model Specification
% ============================================================
\begin{frame}{Optimal Model Specification: Why a BVAR?}
\label{slide:bvar}

\textbf{\footnotesize Why a Bayesian Vector Autoregression?}

\scriptsize
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Captures simultaneous dynamics across the New-Keynesian triplet}\\
        The triple $(\Delta y,\,\pi,\,i)$ spans the canonical NK three-equation system. For Spain, the long-term rate reflects euro-area monetary policy and sovereign risk premia, so the model traces the transmission of financial conditions to domestic activity.

  \item \textbf{Bayesian shrinkage addresses the estimation variance problem}\\
        With 100 quarterly observations and 21 reduced-form parameters, the unrestricted OLS VAR faces over-fitting. The Minnesota prior anchors the posterior toward a parsimonious random-walk benchmark. It preserves the multivariate cross-equation information while regularising the coefficients.

  \item \textbf{Rich policy diagnostics}\\
        Impulse-response functions (Slide~\ref{slide:granger-irf}) confirm that tighter financial conditions (a positive shock to the long-term rate) reduce GDP growth over the subsequent 12 quarters, in line with theoretical priors.

  \item \textbf{Structural-break evidence motivates robust shrinkage, not splitting}\\
        As we will see in Slides~\ref{slide:breaks}--\ref{slide:breaks-interp}, the structural-break tests strongly reject parameter constancy at the 2020 COVID shock. However, this is an exogenous outlier rather than a fundamental regime change. The Minnesota prior naturally absorbs such large innovations without requiring a fragile time-varying-parameter specification or splitting the sample.
\end{enumerate}

\end{frame}

    \textbf{Two practical implications:}\\[0.3em]
    \textbf{1. Sample retention.} Because the pre-2020 dynamics appear relatively stable save for the GFC turbulence, we keep the \textbf{full 2001Q1--2025Q4 sample} to estimate all models. Splitting the sample would discard vital history and cripple estimation precision.\\[0.3em]
    \textbf{2. COVID treatment.} The 2020 observation is a \textbf{one-off exogenous shock} rather than a regime change in the parameter vector. We do not include a permanent dummy or estimate a time-varying-parameter model. We rely on the Minnesota prior of the BVAR to provide robust shrinkage and prevent the outlier from completely distorting the parameters.
  \end{column}

\begin{frame}{Economic interpretation and conclusion}
\label{slide:conclusion}

\begin{block}{Main conclusions}
\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
  \item Spain's GDP growth exhibits strong short-run persistence, but is heavily disrupted by the COVID-19 shock.
  \item Inflation and long-term interest rate lags jointly improve the forecasting performance within a BVAR framework, consistent with a delayed macro-financial transmission.
  \item The BVAR(2) outperforms the required AR(2) and VAR(2) benchmarks by RMSE/MAE; an equal-weight forecast combination performs best or competitively overall.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Caveat}
\footnotesize
The forecast ranking is numerically clear but not statistically decisive under Diebold-Mariano tests. Diagnostics warn against over-interpreting OLS assumptions (like normality) due to extreme crisis observations, so the strongest claim here is relative forecasting performance, not structural causality.
\end{block}
