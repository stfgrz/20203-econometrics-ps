1 Introduction

This report specifies and evaluates an econometric model for the year-on-year real GDP growth rate of Spain using quarterly data. Following the structure required by the assignment, the analysis proceeds in six steps: (i) descriptive analysis of the variables; (ii) a discussion of alternative specifications; (iii) diagnostic checks on the preferred model; (iv) tests of economic hypotheses on the model parameters; (v) a one-step-ahead pseudo-out-of-sample forecasting exercise over the last ten years (40 quarters) of the sample; and (vi) a comparison with the AR(2) and VAR(2) benchmarks prescribed by the brief, where the VAR also includes year-on-year HICP inflation and a short-term interest rate.

We work with quarterly observations from the Eurostat data provided in the ESdata.xlsx file, which contains seasonally-adjusted quarterly real GDP and the monthly HICP index (aggregated to quarterly averages). The short-term interest rate is the 3-month EURIBOR (the euro-area money-market rate), sourced from the ECB Data Portal and aggregated to quarterly averages. We restrict the sample to 2001Q1–2025Q4 — the period for which year-on-year growth rates can be computed from the available quarterly GDP series (which begins in 2000Q1). The resulting sample provides 100 quarterly year-on-year growth observations, sufficient for the AR(2), ARIMA, VAR(2), and BVAR(2) models considered below.

The stationarity evidence for year-on-year GDP growth at quarterly frequency is assessed jointly by the ADF and KPSS tests. Following the macroeconomic literature, we keep the variables in levels rather than differencing them, since (a) the Bayesian VAR’s Minnesota prior shrinks coefficients toward a random walk and is robust to persistent regressors, and (b) the short-term interest rate and inflation are theoretically bounded and cointegration with growth is plausible.

4 Structural Stability and Breakpoint Tests

The descriptive evidence — the level shift in the rolling mean of GDP growth during the GFC and COVID episodes, and the spike in rolling volatility — points to potential structural change in the joint data-generating process. We assess this systematically with (i) Chow \(F\)-tests at three economically motivated candidate dates, (ii) the Quandt–Andrews sup-\(F\) test, which leaves the break date unknown, and (iii) the Bai–Perron multiple-breakpoint procedure. The CUSUM test of recursive residuals is reported as a complementary visual diagnostic.

4.1 Chow tests at three candidate breakpoints

The three candidates were chosen a priori from the historical record of post-2000 Spanish macro shocks:

  • CH1 (2008Q1) — onset of the Global Financial Crisis.
  • CH2 (2012Q3) — Draghi’s “whatever it takes” / OMT announcement.
  • CH3 (2020Q2) — COVID-19 pandemic shock (the most severe quarterly contraction).

Under \(H_0\) the AR(2) coefficients \((c, \phi_1, \phi_2)\) are constant across the two sub-samples defined by the candidate date.

Code 
chow_at <- function(date_break) {
    pt <- which.min(abs(df_ar$date - date_break))
    if (length(pt) == 0 || pt < 4 || pt > nrow(df_ar) - 4) {
        return(c(F = NA_real_, p = NA_real_))
    }
    res <- strucchange::sctest(y ~ y1 + y2, data = df_ar,
                               type = "Chow", point = pt)
    c(F = unname(res$statistic), p = unname(res$p.value))
}

4.3 Breakpoint Analysis: Interpretation

The breakpoint analysis on quarterly data produces results broadly consistent with the annual analysis. The COVID-19 shock of 2020Q2 is the most disruptive event for the AR(2) parameters, while the GFC produces marginal rejections. The Quandt–Andrews and Bai–Perron procedures pinpoint the instability around the GFC/COVID episodes.

Two practical implications follow for the modelling strategy:

  1. Sample retention. We keep the full 2001Q1–2025Q4 sample to estimate all models, since the pre-2020 dynamics appear relatively stable.
  2. COVID treatment. The 2020Q2 observation is a one-off, exogenous shock rather than a regime change. We therefore do not include a permanent dummy or estimate a time-varying-parameter specification. Instead we
    1. report forecast accuracy with and without the COVID quarters in the evaluation window and (ii) note that the Minnesota prior of the BVAR — which shrinks toward a random walk — is the natural device for partly absorbing such large innovations without over-parameterising the model.

5 Cointegration Analysis

Although GDP growth is treated as \(I(0)\) (Section 3.5), inflation and the short-term interest rate behave as persistent, near-unit-root processes over our sample. We therefore investigate whether these series share one or more long-run equilibrium relationships, using the standard sequence of Engle–Granger residual-based tests followed by the Johansen system test.

6.3 Economic Rationale for the Optimal Specification

Beyond the statistical arguments above, four economic considerations support the BVAR(2) as the optimal specification for Spanish real-GDP growth.

1. The variable set captures the dominant transmission channels of a small open economy in EMU. The triple \((\Delta y, \pi, i)\) is the canonical core of a New-Keynesian three-equation system: aggregate demand (driven by the real interest rate), an inflation-augmented Phillips curve, and a monetary-policy reaction function. For Spain — a price-taker in EMU since 1999 — the short-term rate (3-month EURIBOR) directly reflects the euro-area monetary-policy stance, and adding it lets the model trace the transmission of monetary and financial conditions to domestic activity. The cross-correlation analysis (Section 3.3) and the cointegration evidence (Section 5) both support this triplet as economically meaningful.

2. Two lags align the model with the propagation horizon of the relevant shocks. Macroeconomic theory and a long empirical literature suggest that monetary impulses transmit to real activity within roughly 4–8 quarters; on quarterly data two lags captures one to two quarters of direct dynamics, with the year-on-year overlap in the dependent variable providing additional implicit memory. The information criteria help guide the choice, but \(p = 2\) keeps a cushion of dynamic flexibility without descending into overfitting territory.

3. Bayesian shrinkage is the right answer to the estimation problem. With 100 quarterly observations and 21 reduced-form parameters in the VAR(2) plus a \(3\times 3\) residual covariance, the unrestricted MLE is known to suffer from large estimation variance and spurious in-sample fit. The Minnesota prior anchors the posterior toward a parsimonious random-walk benchmark, exploiting two stylised facts that hold particularly well for our series: (i) own lags carry most of the predictive content; (ii) higher-order lags add little signal beyond noise.

4. The structural-stability evidence supports a single, time-invariant specification. As shown in Section 4, parameter constancy is rejected primarily at the COVID date, and that episode is best treated as an exogenous outlier rather than a regime change. There is therefore no case for switching, time-varying-parameter, or split-sample specifications.

In short, the BVAR(2) is well motivated a priori because it is economically complete (it spans the demand–supply–policy triangle), dynamically adequate (two lags cover the relevant transmission horizon), statistically disciplined (Minnesota shrinkage delivers favourable bias–variance trade-offs), and consistent with the structural evidence (no regime change requiring richer specifications).

9.4 Discussion of the forecast comparison

Three sets of results — point accuracy (Section 7.1), tests of equal predictive accuracy (Section 7.2), and interval calibration (this subsection) — together inform the ranking of the four candidate specifications.

Point accuracy. The models are ranked by RMSE and MAE over the forty-quarter (ten-year) window. The parsimonious ARIMA forecasts best — the only model with Theil’s \(U<1\) — followed by the equal-weight combination; the richer VAR(2) and BVAR(2) rank last, heavily penalised by the COVID quarters inside the evaluation window.

Statistical significance. The Diebold–Mariano test assesses whether accuracy differences are statistically significant. With forty one-quarter forecasts the test still has limited power, and the presence of COVID-affected quarters inflates the variance of the loss differential.

Interval calibration. Empirical coverage of the 90% predictive interval is an additional accuracy metric: a well-calibrated model should cover the realised value about 9 times out of 10.

Why the ranking comes out this way. The COVID observations dominate the squared-error loss and destabilise the recursively re-estimated multivariate models: with 100 quarterly observations and 21 parameters the OLS VAR(2) pays the largest variance penalty, and even the Minnesota-shrunk BVAR(2) cannot fully absorb the 2020 outliers (echoing Lenza & Primiceri, 2022, and Carriero et al., 2024). The univariate ARIMA, and the equal-weight combination that diversifies across models, are the most robust. Excluding the COVID quarters the five models are near-tied (RMSE 4.2–4.5), confirming that the gap is an outlier phenomenon rather than evidence of a uniformly superior specification.

10 Conclusion

The analysis specifies and evaluates four competing models for the year-on-year real GDP growth rate of Spain over 2001Q1–2025Q4 using quarterly data from Eurostat. The descriptive evidence (autocorrelation pattern, KPSS, ADF, rolling moments) is consistent with a persistent but bounded process for \(\Delta y_t\), while inflation and the short-term interest rate display the persistence typical of euro-area macro variables. Pre-specified Chow tests, the Quandt–Andrews sup-\(F\), and the Bai–Perron procedure assess parameter constancy, with the COVID-19 episode representing the most disruptive event — which we therefore treat as an exogenous outlier rather than as a regime change. The Engle–Granger and Johansen Trace tests provide a full-rank (near-stationary) system at the 5% level rather than classic reduced-rank cointegration, which supports the level-VAR/BVAR approach.

The required AR(2) and VAR(2) models satisfy the standard residual diagnostics and the VAR(2) is covariance-stationary. Block Granger-causality tests probe the predictive relationships among the three variables.

In the one-step-ahead pseudo-out-of-sample forecasting exercise, the Bayesian VAR(2) — our a priori multivariate benchmark — is evaluated against the AR(2), ARIMA, and unrestricted VAR(2). Over the COVID-dominated ten-year window the parsimonious ARIMA and the equal-weight combination forecast most accurately, while the VAR(2) and BVAR(2) are penalised by the 2020 outliers; no Diebold–Mariano test rejects equal predictive accuracy, so the ranking is suggestive rather than decisive. # Structural Stability and Breakpoint Tests

The descriptive evidence — the level shift in the rolling mean of GDP growth
during the GFC and COVID episodes, and the spike in rolling volatility — points
to potential structural change in the joint data-generating process. We assess
this systematically with (i) Chow $F$-tests at three economically motivated
candidate dates, (ii) the Quandt--Andrews sup-$F$ test, which leaves the break
date *unknown*, and (iii) the Bai--Perron multiple-breakpoint procedure. The
CUSUM test of recursive residuals is reported as a complementary visual diagnostic.

## Chow tests at three candidate breakpoints

The three candidates were chosen *a priori* from the historical record of
post-2000 Spanish macro shocks:

- **CH1 (2008Q1)** — onset of the Global Financial Crisis.
- **CH2 (2012Q3)** — Draghi's "whatever it takes" / OMT announcement.
- **CH3 (2020Q2)** — COVID-19 pandemic shock (the most severe quarterly contraction).

Under $H_0$ the AR(2) coefficients $(c, \phi_1, \phi_2)$ are constant across
the two sub-samples defined by the candidate date.

## Breakpoint Analysis: Interpretation

The breakpoint analysis on quarterly data produces results broadly consistent
with the annual analysis. The COVID-19 shock of 2020Q2 is the most
disruptive event for the AR(2) parameters, while the GFC produces marginal
rejections. The Quandt--Andrews and Bai--Perron procedures pinpoint the
instability around the GFC/COVID episodes.

Two practical implications follow for the modelling strategy:

1. *Sample retention.* We keep the full 2001Q1--2025Q4 sample to estimate all
   models, since the pre-2020 dynamics appear relatively stable.
2. *COVID treatment.* The 2020Q2 observation is a *one-off*, exogenous
   shock rather than a regime change. We therefore *do not* include a permanent
   dummy or estimate a time-varying-parameter specification. Instead we
   (i) report forecast accuracy with and without the COVID quarters in the
   evaluation window and (ii) note that the Minnesota prior of the BVAR —
   which shrinks toward a random walk — is the natural device for partly
   absorbing such large innovations without over-parameterising the model.

# Cointegration Analysis

Although GDP growth is treated as $I(0)$ (Section 3.5), inflation and the
short-term interest rate behave as persistent, near-unit-root processes over our
sample. We therefore investigate whether these series share one or more
long-run equilibrium relationships, using the standard sequence of
Engle--Granger residual-based tests followed by the Johansen system test.

## Engle--Granger ADF on the cointegrating regression residuals

We run the static OLS cointegrating regression
$\Delta y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \pi_t + \beta_2 i_t + u_t$
and test whether $\hat u_t$ is stationary. Because the regressors are
estimated, conventional ADF reference distributions do not apply exactly;
accordingly, we report the Engle--Granger residual ADF test statistic
together with the critical values returned by `urca::ur.df`.

```{r engle-granger}
eg_data <- as.data.frame(var_data)

eg_res  <- residuals(eg_reg)

eg_adf <- urca::ur.df(eg_res, type = "none", lags = 8, selectlags = "BIC")
eg_stat <- eg_adf@teststat[1, "tau1"]
eg_crit <- eg_adf@cval["tau1", c("1pct", "5pct", "10pct")]

eg_tab <- data.frame(
    Statistic = c("ADF (no constant) on EG residuals",
                  "1% critical value", "5% critical value", "10% critical value"),
    Value     = c(eg_stat, eg_crit)

     caption = "Engle--Granger residual-based cointegration test (quarterly)",
     latex_file = "engle_granger_q")
```

## Johansen Trace test (no constant, no trend)

The Johansen procedure jointly tests for the cointegration rank $r$ in the
VECM representation
$\Delta \mathbf{x}_t = \Pi \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p-1} \Gamma_j
\Delta \mathbf{x}_{t-j} + \mathbf{u}_t$, with $\Pi = \alpha \beta'$. We use the
**transitory** specification with `ecdet = "none"` (no constant or trend in
either the cointegrating relation or outside) and $K = 2$ to match the lag
length of the VAR(2).

```{r johansen}
joh <- urca::ca.jo(var_data, type = "trace", ecdet = "none",
                   K = 2, spec = "transitory")

joh_tab <- data.frame(
    `H0: rank <= r` = paste0("r <= ", c(2, 1, 0)),
    `Trace stat`   = joh@teststat,
    `10%`          = joh@cval[, "10pct"],
    `5%`           = joh@cval[, "5pct"],
    `1%`           = joh@cval[, "1pct"],
    check.names    = FALSE
)
ftab(joh_tab, digits = 3,
     caption = "Johansen Trace test (no constant, no trend, K = 2) -- quarterly",
     latex_file = "johansen_q")
```

## Estimated cointegrating vector and loading matrix

Conditional on the rank suggested by the trace test, we estimate the VECM
with restricted constant via `cajorls` and recover the cointegrating
vector $\beta$ and the loading (adjustment) matrix $\alpha$. The latter
governs the speed at which each variable corrects deviations from the
long-run equilibrium $\beta' \mathbf{x}_{t-1}$.

```{r vecm-loadings}
n_reject <- as.integer(sum(joh@teststat > joh@cval[, "5pct"]))
K_var    <- ncol(var_data)
full_rank <- n_reject >= K_var
# Rank used to estimate the VECM, capped at the maximal reduced rank (K - 1).
chosen_r <- min(max(n_reject, 0L), K_var - 1L)
loadings_summary <- NA_character_
loadings_text <- "No loading-matrix adjustment summary is reported because r = 0."

  vecm <- urca::cajorls(joh, r = chosen_r)

  beta_hat <- as.matrix(vecm$beta)
  colnames(beta_hat) <- paste0("ECT", seq_len(chosen_r))
  alpha_hat <- t(coef(vecm$rlm)[paste0("ect", seq_len(chosen_r)), , drop = FALSE])
  rownames(alpha_hat) <- c("GDP growth", "Inflation", "ST rate")
  colnames(alpha_hat) <- paste0("ECT", seq_len(chosen_r))

  dominant_eq <- apply(abs(alpha_hat), 2, function(x) rownames(alpha_hat)[which.max(x)])
  loadings_summary <- paste(
    paste0(colnames(alpha_hat), " \u2192 ", dominant_eq),
```

## Economic Rationale for the Optimal Specification

Beyond the statistical arguments above, four economic considerations support
the BVAR(2) as the optimal specification for Spanish real-GDP growth.

**1. The variable set captures the dominant transmission channels of a small
open economy in EMU.** The triple $(\Delta y, \pi, i)$ is the canonical
core of a New-Keynesian three-equation system: aggregate demand (driven by
the real interest rate), an inflation-augmented Phillips curve, and a
monetary-policy reaction function. For Spain — a price-taker in EMU since
1999 — the short-term rate (3-month EURIBOR) directly reflects the euro-area
monetary-policy stance, and adding it lets the model trace the transmission
of monetary and financial conditions to domestic activity. The cross-correlation
analysis (Section 3.3) and the cointegration evidence (Section 5) both
support this triplet as economically meaningful.

**2. Two lags align the model with the propagation horizon of the
relevant shocks.** Macroeconomic theory and a long empirical literature
suggest that monetary impulses transmit to real activity within roughly
4--8 quarters; on quarterly data two lags captures one to two quarters of
direct dynamics, with the year-on-year overlap in the dependent variable
providing additional implicit memory. The information criteria help guide
the choice, but $p = 2$ keeps a cushion of dynamic flexibility without
descending into overfitting territory.

**3. Bayesian shrinkage is the right answer to the estimation problem.**
With `r n_obs` quarterly observations and 21 reduced-form parameters in
the VAR(2) plus a $3\times 3$ residual covariance, the unrestricted MLE
is known to suffer from large estimation variance and spurious in-sample
fit. The Minnesota prior anchors the posterior toward a parsimonious
random-walk benchmark, exploiting two stylised facts that hold
particularly well for our series: (i) own lags carry most of the
predictive content; (ii) higher-order lags add little signal beyond noise.

**4. The structural-stability evidence supports a single, time-invariant
specification.** As shown in Section 4, parameter constancy is rejected
primarily at the COVID date, and that episode is best treated as an
exogenous outlier rather than a regime change. There is therefore no case
for switching, time-varying-parameter, or split-sample specifications.

In short, the BVAR(2) is well motivated *a priori* because it is *economically complete*
(it spans the demand--supply--policy triangle), *dynamically adequate*
(two lags cover the relevant transmission horizon), *statistically
disciplined* (Minnesota shrinkage delivers favourable bias--variance
trade-offs), and *consistent with the structural evidence* (no regime
change requiring richer specifications).

# One-step-ahead Forecasting Evaluation

We evaluate the four models on a pseudo-out-of-sample exercise covering the
last ten years (40 quarters) of the sample. Forecasts are generated with an **expanding
window**: at each origin $T = N - 40, \ldots, N - 1$, each model is re-estimated
on data up to $T$ and used to predict $\Delta y_{T+1}$.

```{r forecasting-loop, results='hide'}
h_out   <- 40
n_total <- nrow(var_data)
n_train <- n_total - h_out
ci_lvl  <- 0.90

models <- c("BVAR", "VAR2", "AR2", "ARIMA", "Combined")
arr <- array(NA_real_,
    dim = c(h_out, length(models), 3),
    dimnames = list(NULL, models, c("fcst", "lo", "hi"))
)
actuals  <- as.numeric(var_data[(n_train + 1):n_total, "GDP_Growth"])
dates_oos <- spain$date[(n_train + 1):n_total]

q_lo <- (1 - ci_lvl) / 2
q_hi <- 1 - q_lo

for (i in seq_len(h_out)) {
    end_t       <- n_train + i - 1
    train_multi <- var_data[1:end_t, , drop = FALSE]
    train_uni   <- ts_gdp[1:end_t]

    # BVAR(2)
    set.seed(20203 + i)
    b_fit  <- BVAR::bvar(train_multi, lags = 2,
                         n_draw = 4000, n_burn = 1000, verbose = FALSE)
    b_pred <- predict(b_fit, horizon = 1)
    bdraws <- b_pred$fcast[, 1, 1]
    arr[i, "BVAR", ] <- c(median(bdraws),
                          unname(quantile(bdraws, q_lo)),
                          unname(quantile(bdraws, q_hi)))

    # VAR(2)
    v_fit  <- vars::VAR(train_multi, p = 2, type = "const")
    v_pred <- predict(v_fit, n.ahead = 1, ci = ci_lvl)$fcst$GDP_Growth
    arr[i, "VAR2", ] <- c(v_pred[1, "fcst"], v_pred[1, "lower"], v_pred[1, "upper"])

    # AR(2)
    ar_fit <- forecast::Arima(train_uni, order = c(2, 0, 0))
    a_fc   <- forecast::forecast(ar_fit, h = 1, level = ci_lvl * 100)
    arr[i, "AR2", ] <- c(as.numeric(a_fc$mean[1]),
                         as.numeric(a_fc$lower[1, 1]),
                         as.numeric(a_fc$upper[1, 1]))

    # ARIMA selected by BIC on the training sample
    arima_fit <- forecast::auto.arima(train_uni, ic = "bic",
                                      stationary = TRUE, seasonal = FALSE)
    ar_fc     <- forecast::forecast(arima_fit, h = 1, level = ci_lvl * 100)
    arr[i, "ARIMA", ] <- c(as.numeric(ar_fc$mean[1]),
                           as.numeric(ar_fc$lower[1, 1]),
                           as.numeric(ar_fc$upper[1, 1]))

    # Combined forecast (equal weight of BVAR, VAR2, AR2, ARIMA)
    arr[i, "Combined", "fcst"] <- mean(c(arr[i, "BVAR", "fcst"], arr[i, "VAR2", "fcst"], arr[i, "AR2", "fcst"], arr[i, "ARIMA", "fcst"]))
    # Approximate CI by averaging the bounds (for illustration)
    arr[i, "Combined", "lo"] <- mean(c(arr[i, "BVAR", "lo"], arr[i, "VAR2", "lo"], arr[i, "AR2", "lo"], arr[i, "ARIMA", "lo"]))
    arr[i, "Combined", "hi"] <- mean(c(arr[i, "BVAR", "hi"], arr[i, "VAR2", "hi"], arr[i, "AR2", "hi"], arr[i, "ARIMA", "hi"]))
}

forecasts <- arr[, , "fcst"]
fcst_lo   <- arr[, , "lo"]
```

## Discussion of the forecast comparison

Three sets of results — point accuracy (Section 7.1), tests of equal
predictive accuracy (Section 7.2), and interval calibration (this
subsection) — together inform the ranking of the four candidate
specifications.

**Point accuracy.** The models are ranked by RMSE and MAE over the
forty-quarter (ten-year) window. The parsimonious ARIMA forecasts best — the
only model with Theil's $U<1$ — followed by the equal-weight combination; the
richer VAR(2) and BVAR(2) rank last, heavily penalised by the COVID quarters
inside the evaluation window.

**Statistical significance.** The Diebold--Mariano test assesses whether
accuracy differences are statistically significant. With forty
one-quarter forecasts the test still has limited power, and the presence of
COVID-affected quarters inflates the variance of the loss differential.

**Interval calibration.** Empirical coverage of the 90% predictive
interval is an additional accuracy metric: a well-calibrated model should
cover the realised value about 9 times out of 10.

**Why the ranking comes out this way.** The COVID observations dominate the
squared-error loss and destabilise the recursively re-estimated multivariate
models: with `r n_obs` quarterly observations and 21 parameters the OLS VAR(2)
pays the largest variance penalty, and even the Minnesota-shrunk BVAR(2) cannot
fully absorb the 2020 outliers (echoing Lenza & Primiceri, 2022, and Carriero et
al., 2024). The univariate ARIMA, and the equal-weight combination that
diversifies across models, are the most robust. Excluding the COVID quarters the
five models are near-tied (RMSE 4.2--4.5), confirming that the gap is an outlier
phenomenon rather than evidence of a uniformly superior specification.

# Conclusion

The analysis specifies and evaluates four competing models for the
year-on-year real GDP growth rate of Spain over 2001Q1--2025Q4 using quarterly
data from Eurostat. The descriptive evidence (autocorrelation pattern, KPSS,
ADF, rolling moments) is consistent with a persistent but bounded process for
$\Delta y_t$, while inflation and the short-term interest rate display the
persistence typical of euro-area macro variables. Pre-specified Chow tests, the
Quandt--Andrews sup-$F$, and the Bai--Perron procedure assess parameter
constancy, with the COVID-19 episode representing the most disruptive event —
which we therefore treat as an exogenous outlier rather than as a regime change.
The Engle--Granger and Johansen Trace tests provide
`r conclusion_cointegration_text`

The required AR(2) and VAR(2) models satisfy the standard residual
diagnostics and the VAR(2) is covariance-stationary. Block Granger-causality
tests probe the predictive relationships among the three variables.

In the one-step-ahead pseudo-out-of-sample forecasting exercise, the
**Bayesian VAR(2)** — our *a priori* multivariate benchmark — is evaluated
against the AR(2), ARIMA, and unrestricted VAR(2). Over the COVID-dominated
ten-year window the parsimonious ARIMA and the equal-weight combination forecast
most accurately, while the VAR(2) and BVAR(2) are penalised by the 2020 outliers;
no Diebold--Mariano test rejects equal predictive accuracy, so the ranking is
suggestive rather than decisive. Diebold--Mariano tests are reported over the
forty-quarter (ten-year) evaluation window.
